Chronic Kidney Disease

A gradual, long-term loss of kidney function — understanding the condition early makes the biggest difference to how it's managed.

Nephrology Long-Term Condition Manageable with Early Care

What is Chronic Kidney Disease?

Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is the gradual loss of kidney function over time. The kidneys filter waste and excess fluid from the blood; when this filtering ability declines slowly over months or years, waste can build up in the body. Because early CKD often causes no noticeable symptoms, it is frequently picked up through routine blood and urine tests rather than through how a person feels.

Causes & Risk Factors

CKD usually develops as a consequence of another long-term condition rather than on its own. Common contributors include:

  • Long-standing diabetes (the single most common cause)
  • High blood pressure, which gradually damages the small blood vessels in the kidneys
  • Recurrent kidney infections or long-standing kidney stones
  • Glomerulonephritis and other inflammatory kidney conditions
  • Prolonged urinary tract obstruction
  • A family history of kidney disease

Symptoms to Watch For

Symptoms tend to appear only once kidney function has dropped significantly, which is why screening matters for anyone at risk. Watch for:

  • Persistent tiredness or low energy
  • Swelling in the legs, ankles or around the eyes
  • Changes in urination — frequency, foaminess or colour
  • Persistent itching or nausea
  • Poor appetite and unexplained weight loss
  • Difficulty concentrating or disturbed sleep

If you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or a family history of kidney disease, periodic kidney function screening is recommended even without symptoms.

Stages of CKD

CKD is staged by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), a measure of how well the kidneys are filtering:

StageeGFR RangeDescription
Stage 190+Normal function, but other signs of kidney damage present
Stage 260–89Mild reduction in function
Stage 330–59Moderate reduction; most commonly diagnosed stage
Stage 415–29Severe reduction; preparation for renal replacement therapy begins
Stage 5<15Kidney failure; dialysis or transplantation typically required

Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines a few straightforward tests rather than any single check:

  • Blood test to calculate eGFR from serum creatinine
  • Urine test to check for albumin or protein leakage
  • Imaging (ultrasound) to assess kidney size and structure
  • Blood pressure monitoring, since hypertension is both a cause and a consequence

Treatment Options

Treatment is aimed at slowing progression and managing the underlying cause, since kidney tissue already lost cannot be regenerated:

  • Managing the underlying condition — tighter control of blood sugar and blood pressure
  • Medication to protect kidney function and manage complications such as anaemia or bone health
  • Dietary and lifestyle changes — salt and protein moderation, guided by a dietitian
  • Regular monitoring of kidney function to track progression
  • Dialysis for advanced kidney failure (Stage 5)
  • Kidney transplantation as a long-term option for eligible patients

When to See a Specialist

A referral to a urologist or nephrologist is worthwhile if blood or urine tests show reduced kidney function, if you have risk factors such as diabetes or hypertension, or if you notice any of the symptoms listed above. Earlier evaluation gives more options for slowing progression.
