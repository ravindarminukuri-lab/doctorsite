Bladder Cancer

Robotic radical cystectomy, urinary diversion and organ-preserving strategies for non-muscle and muscle-invasive disease.

Bladder cancer is more common in adults over 55 but can occur in younger people too. Treatment depends heavily on whether the cancer is confined to the bladder lining (non-muscle-invasive) or has grown into the muscle wall (muscle-invasive), and Dr. Agrawal tailors the approach to each category.

Bladder-Preserving Options

TURBT and intravesical therapy considered first for non-muscle-invasive disease.

Robotic Cystectomy

Robotic technique for more precise bladder removal when needed.

Reconstruction Planning

Urinary diversion or neobladder options discussed and planned in advance.

Signs to Watch For

  • Blood in the urine, often without pain
  • Frequent or urgent need to urinate
  • Pain or burning during urination
  • Pelvic pain in more advanced disease

Diagnosis

Diagnosis usually involves urine tests, imaging and cystoscopy (a camera examination of the bladder), with a biopsy (TURBT) to confirm the tumour type and how deep it extends into the bladder wall.

Treatment Options

  • Bladder-preserving treatment (TURBT and intravesical therapy) for non-muscle-invasive disease
  • Robotic radical cystectomy with urinary diversion or neobladder reconstruction for muscle-invasive disease
  • Organ-preserving strategies where oncologically appropriate
  • Coordinated multimodal treatment alongside chemotherapy when indicated

Recovery & Outlook

Recovery depends on whether treatment was bladder-preserving or involved cystectomy with reconstruction. Robotic cystectomy patients are supported through a structured recovery pathway, with close attention to the new urinary diversion or neobladder during the initial weeks.

  • Structured pathway for adapting to a diversion or neobladder
  • Regular cystoscopy follow-up for non-muscle-invasive disease
  • Coordination with oncology for combined treatment when needed
